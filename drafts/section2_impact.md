## 2.1 Credibility of Measures to Enhance Career Perspectives and Employability

**Scientific backdrop**: this project sits at the intersection of several active fields — ancient horse genomics and domestication science, island biogeography and the genomics of isolation, aDNA methodology, and Norse/Viking Age history. No ancient genomic data currently exist for horses from Iceland or the wider Norse Atlantic world; this project fills that gap and, via comparison with Orkney/Shetland/Greenland material where possible, connects directly to debates about the composition and selectivity of Norse settlement-era animal transport.

**Career trajectory**: the applicant's career to date has been built on ancient human population genomics at deCODE Genetics, culminating in a PhD (2023) and the 2018 *Science* paper on the founding of the Icelandic human population. This fellowship represents a deliberate expansion from human to non-human ancient genomics, applying a proven methodological toolkit (aDNA extraction, GLIMPSE2 imputation, IBD analysis) to a new taxonomic and analytical domain (breed-structured domestic animal genomics, functional/trait genetics) — a direction motivated not opportunistically, but by a personal engagement with the Icelandic horse dating back to childhood (see Section 1.4). This broadens the applicant's competitiveness for independent research positions across both human and non-human ancient genomics, including future PI-track grant applications of the kind she has already begun contributing to as a named collaborator (e.g. Agnar Helgason's 2027 Icelandic Research Fund project grant on Icelandic horse genomics). Acquiring skills in demographic modelling (PSMC/SMC++), admixture graph modelling in a multi-breed context, and functional trait genetics — none previously part of the applicant's toolkit — directly increases her ability to lead such projects independently in the future.

A Career Development Plan, agreed with Prof. Orlando at project start and reviewed at regular intervals, will formalise these training objectives and track progress against them (see also Section 1.3).

**Societal and economic relevance**: the Icelandic horse is a living cultural and economic resource — approximately 80,000–100,000 registered horses in Iceland and over 100,000 internationally, one of the most internationally distributed native breeds in the world. Results will provide the first population-level baseline of genomic diversity from which current genetic health can be assessed; identify historical variants associated with heritable conditions (sweet itch, metabolic syndrome-like profiles, ocular conditions) relevant to modern breeding strategies; and clarify the genomic basis of the tölt and pace gaits, traits central to breed identity and competitive use. The approach developed here — quantifying genomic consequences of long-term isolation — is also transferable to other isolated or endangered domestic and wild populations, informing conservation breeding programmes more broadly (cf. Lovász et al. 2025 on rewilded-horse conservation genetics).

## 2.3 Magnitude and Importance of the Project's Contribution to Expected Scientific, Societal, and Economic Impacts

**Scientific impact**: this project adds the first ancient genomic dataset (~90 individuals, 1,100-year transect) for a domestic animal population that is currently entirely absent from the global ancient horse genomic record, directly addressing a named gap in the field (Section 1.1). In magnitude, this represents a ~10% increase in total published ancient horse genomes from the North Atlantic/Norse world (currently effectively zero for Iceland specifically), and in importance, it provides the reference dataset against which any future ancient DNA work on North Atlantic horse populations (Faroes, Greenland, Orkney/Shetland) will necessarily be compared.

**Societal impact**: results will directly inform breeding and health management decisions for a population of ~180,000–200,000 living animals (Iceland and international registries combined) by providing the first genomic baseline for assessing current genetic health and the historical origin of heritable conditions (sweet itch, metabolic syndrome-like profiles, ocular conditions) affecting a meaningful fraction of the breed. Given the breed's international distribution, findings are directly transferable to breeding association practice in Germany, Scandinavia, and North America, not just Iceland.

**Economic impact**: while no commercial exploitation is anticipated directly from this project, the Icelandic horse is a breed of substantial direct economic importance (export value, tourism, competitive sport); a genomically-informed baseline for breeding decisions has direct downstream relevance to breed value and health-related veterinary costs, though quantifying this precisely is beyond the scope of this fellowship.

Overall, the clearest and most directly attributable impact is scientific (a permanent, reusable dataset filling a specific, named gap) and societal (breed health/conservation), with economic impact real but indirect.
