## 1.1 Objectives and Research Questions

The Icelandic horse is one of the most genetically isolated domestic animal populations in the world. Since Norse settlers brought the first horses to Iceland in the late 9th century CE, the population has remained closed to outside breeding for over a millennium, preserved by one of the oldest livestock import bans in recorded history (formalised in law in 1882, and still in force today). Despite this uniquely continuous and well-documented lineage, the ancient genomic history of the Icelandic horse has never been investigated at the resolution required for modern population genomic analysis. No genome-wide study has yet reconstructed how this founding population was established, how it diversified over time, or how sustained geographic isolation shaped its genetic architecture. The only prior aDNA work on this population (Pálsdóttir/Nistelberger et al. 2019) was limited to sexing a small number of specimens, and present-day-only studies using microarray data (Sigurðardóttir et al. 2024) can only infer historical demography indirectly — for example estimating effective population size (N_e) at ~125 today, down from ~500 sixty generations ago, purely by extrapolating backward from modern linkage disequilibrium patterns.

This project will generate the first comprehensive ancient genomic dataset for the Icelandic horse, spanning the settlement period (ca. 870 CE) to the late 19th century, and use it to address three interrelated scientific questions that go beyond what present-day-only data can resolve:

**Objective 1 — Origins and founding**: What was the genomic composition of the horses that arrived with Norse settlers? Were they introduced in a single founding event or through multiple waves? What Scandinavian and Eurasian populations contributed to the founding stock, and in what proportions?

**Objective 2 — Post-founding demography**: How did the Icelandic horse population change over 1,100 years of isolation? What is the trajectory of inbreeding, genetic diversity, and effective population size through time — measured directly from ancient genomes via IBD sharing, rather than inferred backward from present-day data alone — and how does this compare to other isolated breeds?

**Objective 3 — Adaptation and trait evolution**: What genomic signatures of local adaptation and selective breeding are detectable in ancient and historical Icelandic horses? How do the genetic variants associated with the breed's most distinctive traits — the tölt and pace gaits (DMRT3, and more recently RELN/STAU2; Sigurðardóttir et al. 2023, 2025), metabolic adaptation to low-calorie winters, and disease susceptibility — evolve through time under isolation?

Together, these objectives will produce the first genome-wide reconstruction of the Icelandic horse's evolutionary history, linking Norse-age animal husbandry with a thousand years of island adaptation, and providing a model system for understanding the genomic consequences of extreme geographic isolation in domestic species.

**Originality**: This project is original in three respects. First, it fills a critical geographic and temporal gap in ancient horse genomics — despite major recent advances in ancient horse genomics (Central Asia, the Pontic steppe, Scandinavia, the Iberian Peninsula), no genomic data exist for the Icelandic horse prior to the modern era, and the 1,100-year temporal depth of this dataset is unmatched for any island horse population. Second, the Icelandic horse is a globally exceptional model system: one of the only domestic animals with a clean, uninterrupted, legally enforced population history free from outside admixture, making it uniquely suited to quantifying the genomic consequences of isolation, founder effects, and drift. Third, the project combines two complementary methodological strengths not previously brought together on this population: the applicant's aDNA extraction and sequencing protocols, optimised for degraded Icelandic skeletal material (Ebenesersdóttir et al., *Science* 2018), and the host laboratory's ancient horse imputation reference panel and analytical framework (Ahmed et al., under review), which together make high-resolution population genomic inference from low-coverage ancient horse genomes feasible for the first time in Iceland.

## 1.2 Methodology

### 1.2.1 Sample collection and ancient DNA extraction

Building on a previously granted sampling permission from the National Museum of Iceland (Þjóðminjasafnið), we will sample up to **90 archaeological horse specimens** spanning the Icelandic settlement period (ca. 870 CE) to ca. 1900 CE, providing a temporal transect of the population across 1,100 years. Specimens include dental remains and cortical bone from well-dated burial contexts (kuml), animal bone deposits, and assemblages from sites across Iceland. We will prioritise teeth (the optimal substrate for ancient DNA preservation), preferentially extracting DNA using silica-based protocols optimised for highly degraded material (Rohland & Hofreiter 2007), supplemented by post-medieval specimens to document later demographic changes.

All aDNA work will be carried out at the dedicated ancient DNA clean-room laboratory at deCODE Genetics, Reykjavík, established and maintained by the applicant. This facility is physically isolated from areas where modern DNA is handled and adheres to strict international contamination-control standards. DNA will be extracted using the same protocols used to generate 201 ancient Icelandic human genomes (Ebenesersdóttir et al., *Science* 2018; manuscript in preparation). In our experience with Icelandic skeletal material, this approach reliably recovers endogenous DNA from specimens over 1,000 years old.

### 1.2.2 Library preparation and sequencing

Double-stranded Illumina sequencing libraries will be prepared with unique dual indexing for multiplexed sequencing, using the single-tube BEST protocol (Carøe et al. 2018) or the single-stranded SCR protocol (Kapp et al. 2021) for the most degraded material. Libraries will undergo initial quality assessment by shallow shotgun sequencing (MiSeq, deCODE Genetics) to determine endogenous DNA content, characteristic ancient DNA damage patterns (cytosine deamination), and library complexity. Samples yielding >1% endogenous horse DNA will proceed to deep sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Novogene; one lane yields ~1000Gb at ~$3,150). We target an average raw sequencing depth of 2–5× per individual prior to imputation, consistent with current best practice in ancient horse genomics.

### 1.2.3 Bioinformatic processing and genotype imputation

Raw reads will be processed using the deCODE Anthropology group's validated aDNA pipeline (adapter trimming with AdapterRemoval2; mapping to the horse reference genome EquCab3 with BWA; duplicate removal with Picard MarkDuplicates; base quality rescaling with mapDamage2). Sex will be estimated from X:Y chromosome read ratios; contamination will be assessed using mtDNA and nuclear methods.

Low-coverage genomes will be imputed to high density using GLIMPSE2 (Rubinacci et al. 2023), leveraging the imputation reference panel of >900 modern and ancient horse genomes developed by Prof. Orlando's laboratory (Ahmed et al., under review). This panel has been validated for imputing ancient horse genomes at low coverage, making it directly applicable to our Icelandic dataset.

### 1.2.4 Population genomic analyses

*Objectives 1 and 2* will be addressed using PCA (Patterson et al. 2006) and ADMIXTURE to characterise ancestry composition relative to modern and ancient horses from Scandinavia, the British Isles, continental Europe, and the Eurasian steppe; f-statistics and admixture graph modelling (ADMIXTOOLS2, Patterson et al. 2012) to formally test ancestry models; identity-by-descent (IBD) analysis using both ancIBD (Ringbauer et al. 2024) and a complementary in-house method (developed by collaborator Agnar Helgason) that detects IBD sharing directly from unphased genotype data, to reconstruct genealogical relationships and track inbreeding through time; runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis; and demographic modelling (PSMC, SMC++) combined with direct N_e estimation from IBD segment length distributions (Browning & Browning 2015) — providing, for the first time, a direct ancient-genome-based estimate of N_e to compare against the present-day-only estimate of ~125 (Sigurðardóttir et al. 2024).

*Objective 3* will be addressed using genome-wide selection scans (XP-EHH, iHS, PBS) comparing ancient Icelandic horses to Eurasian outgroups; targeted genotyping of known functional variants (DMRT3, RELN, STAU2 for gait; ASIP and MC1R for coat colour; variants associated with metabolic traits and immune function); and ROH-based identification of potentially deleterious recessive variants that may have risen in frequency under isolation.

### 1.2.5 Gender dimension and open science

Sex will be determined genetically for all specimens (via X:Y read ratio) and included as a covariate throughout, ensuring balanced representation of both sexes in all analyses; none of the three objectives are expected to show sex-specific effects, but this will be formally tested rather than assumed. All sequencing data generated will be deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under open access conditions upon publication, in accordance with FAIR data principles. All bioinformatic pipelines will be documented and made publicly available via GitHub under open-source licences. We will publish results in open-access journals where possible and make preprints available on bioRxiv prior to formal publication. A data management plan (DMP) will be written at project start and kept updated with the supervisor throughout.

## 1.3 Quality of Supervision, Training, and Two-Way Knowledge Transfer

**Supervisor**: Prof. Ludovic Orlando is a CNRS Silver Medal laureate and founding director of the Centre for Anthropobiology and Genomics of Toulouse (CAGT), University of Toulouse. He is a world leader in ancient horse genomics, having published foundational horse domestication studies in *Nature*, *Science*, and *Cell*, and has developed the reference panel of >900 modern and ancient horse genomes (25.9 million phased genotypes) that underpins this project's imputation strategy. He is co-corresponding author, with the applicant as co-author, on Ahmed et al. (under review, *Science*) — an active, ongoing collaboration that directly demonstrates both the scientific fit and the working relationship between supervisor and applicant.

**Training programme for the fellow**: the applicant brings deep expertise in ancient DNA extraction, human population genomics, and imputation-based analysis (GLIMPSE2, IBD, PCA/ADMIXTURE) from her work on ancient Icelandic humans, but has not previously applied these methods to a non-human, breed-structured population with a large multi-breed comparative reference panel. Under Prof. Orlando's supervision, key learning objectives are: (i) applying and adapting the horse-specific imputation reference panel and its associated QC/validation framework; (ii) admixture graph modelling and formal ancestry testing with ADMIXTOOLS2 in a multi-breed context, distinct from the human-population framing she has used to date; (iii) demographic modelling methods (PSMC, SMC++) not previously used in her human work; (iv) horse-specific functional/trait genetics (coat colour, gait, metabolic loci), an entirely new domain for the applicant. Regular one-on-one meetings with Prof. Orlando, and a Career Development Plan agreed at project start and reviewed throughout, will structure this training.

**Two-way knowledge transfer**: the applicant brings to CAGT aDNA extraction and library preparation protocols specifically optimised for highly degraded sub-Arctic skeletal material (validated on 201 ancient Icelandic human genomes, Ebenesersdóttir et al. *Science* 2018), direct access to Icelandic horse specimens through the National Museum of Iceland and deCODE Genetics (unavailable to the host laboratory otherwise), and applied experience with GLIMPSE2-based imputation and IBD analysis at population scale from the human Iceland2 project — including an in-house unphased-genotype IBD method (developed with collaborator Agnar Helgason) that is complementary to the host laboratory's existing toolkit. This creates genuine bidirectional transfer rather than one-directional training.

**Secondment**: no secondment is planned. The Iceland-based phase of the project (Section 3.1, WP1) constitutes fieldwork and sampling at the applicant's own institution, not a placement at a third organisation, and is therefore distinct from a formal secondment.

## 1.4 Quality and Appropriateness of the Researcher's Professional Experience, Competences and Skills

The applicant holds a PhD in Biological Anthropology (University of Iceland, 2023) and has worked as a Research Scientist at deCODE Genetics since 2013, specialising in ancient DNA and population structure. Her track record directly matches the technical demands of this project: she has independently built and operated deCODE's ancient DNA clean-room laboratory; carried out the full pipeline from sampling through library preparation, sequencing, mapping, variant calling, and imputation (GLIMPSE2); and applied population-genomic methods (PCA, ADMIXTURE, IBD, f-statistics) at the scale of hundreds of ancient genomes. This is demonstrated most directly by Ebenesersdóttir et al. (*Science* 2018), on which she generated and analysed 201 ancient Icelandic human genomes — the largest ancient DNA study of a single population at the time of publication — and by her ongoing contribution to the Iceland2 project (201 ancient Icelandic genomes, manuscript in preparation) and the second plague pandemic study (Xiaodong, Moore, Ebenesersdóttir et al., bioRxiv), both of which required the same imputation- and IBD-based analytical framework proposed for this project.

What is new for this project — and the specific gap this fellowship addresses — is the extension of this expertise from human to non-human, breed-structured genomics, and to functional/trait genetics, both entirely outside her prior publication record. This is precisely the competence gap Prof. Orlando's supervision (Section 1.3) is designed to close, while her existing technical skillset ensures she can execute the laboratory- and pipeline-level work of the project independently from month 1.

Beyond this technical skillset, the applicant brings a long-standing personal engagement with the Icelandic horse specifically, having owned and cared for Icelandic horses since childhood. This gives her a practical, first-hand familiarity with the breed's behaviour, gaits, and temperament — the very traits under investigation in Objective 3 — that is uncommon among researchers approaching horse genomics from a purely computational or laboratory background, and connects her directly and personally to the community of Icelandic horse owners and breeders whose animals and interests this project ultimately serves. Combined with her established technical expertise in ancient DNA, this makes the proposed pivot from human to equine genomics a deliberate, long-considered direction rather than an opportunistic one.
